Project overview

This dashboard presents a computational musicological analysis of my own top songs from 2018 to 2025, using a merged corpus based on my yearly “Your Top Songs” playlists. The aim is to understand both recurring preferences and long-term changes in my listening history.

The project is guided by three research questions:

  1. What are the most common musical features across my top songs?
  2. How do these features evolve over time in my listening habits?
  3. Did my tastes change over the years, and if so, how?

The merged corpus is used for broad comparisons across years, while selected tracks are used for more detailed pitch, timbre, harmonic, and temporal analyses. Together, these different levels of analysis make it possible to compare overall listening patterns with close musical detail.

These pitch self-similarity matrices extend the earlier chromagram and key-based analyses by showing how harmonic material is reused over time within individual songs. Both axes represent time, so each point compares one moment in the song with another. The dark main diagonal from top left to bottom right is expected, because every moment is always maximally similar to itself. The most important places to look are therefore the off-diagonal shapes: when darker paths or blocks appear away from the main diagonal, they suggest that similar pitch material returns at different times. In line with the course material, repeated sections often appear as lines parallel to the main diagonal, while more internally stable or homogeneous sections appear as block-like regions.

Among the three examples, Dolla Dolla Bill shows the clearest repeated sectional structure. The reader can see this most easily in the more visible dark off-diagonal bands and rectangular regions, especially through the middle and later portions of the matrix, which indicate that similar pitch configurations return in multiple places. New Light looks more diffuse: there are still repeated relations, but they are weaker and less cleanly segmented, so the eye catches fewer strong off-diagonal paths and fewer sharply bounded blocks. This makes its form look more continuous and less loop-like. Ojitos Lindos sits between these extremes. It still shows noticeable repeated relations, but they are less rigidly patterned than in Dolla Dolla Bill; the reader should look for broader areas of similarity and partial returns rather than expecting a strongly gridded pattern. Together, these differences suggest that the songs do not simply vary in tonal centre, but also in how clearly and regularly pitch material is repeated across sections.

Taken together, the matrices add a structural perspective to the earlier key and chroma plots. The key heatmap showed broad yearly tonal tendencies, and the chromagrams showed which pitch classes remain active over time; the self-similarity matrices go one step further by showing whether that pitch material is reused in recurring formal units. The reader should therefore compare not just which song has the strongest tonal focus, but also which graph has the clearest off-diagonal returns, since those are the best visual clues for repetition and sectional recurrence. At the same time, these matrices are still feature-based representations rather than direct ground truth about form, so blurred or weak patterns do not mean that repetition is absent altogether; they may also reflect variation in voicing, texture, timing, or harmonic detail between repeated sections.

Taken together, the heatmap, chromagrams, keygrams, and self-similarity matrices suggest that my listening history is shaped not only by recurring tonal centres such as G, B, and Eb/D#, but also by different degrees of tonal stability inside songs: New Light is more fluid, Dolla Dolla Bill is more tightly centred and repetitive, and Ojitos Lindos is tonally focused without being as rigidly patterned.

Loudness in the corpus

This plot suggests that the loudness of my preferred songs was mostly stable over time, since the yearly medians remain in a fairly narrow range and the distributions overlap strongly from 2018 to 2024. That means there is little evidence of a steady shift toward either consistently louder or consistently softer listening across most of the dataset. The main differences instead appear in the spread of the yearly distributions and in the position of the #1 songs. In most years, the favourite track sits close to the centre of that year’s loudness range, but 2025 stands out: its #1 song is much quieter than the yearly core and the year also contains more very quiet outliers. Overall, the figure suggests that loudness was a relatively stable part of my listening profile, while recent years — especially 2025 — may indicate greater openness to softer tracks.

A further point is that this graph distinguishes loudness level from musical intensity more broadly. A track can have a relatively moderate average loudness and still feel powerful because of its arrangement, spectral density, or the way energy is distributed across sections. For that reason, the plot is most useful for showing whether my preferred songs occupy a similar overall loudness range, not for proving that some years were emotionally or sonically “stronger” than others. Read cautiously, the main takeaway is that my listening habits stayed anchored in a fairly consistent loudness profile, while a few quieter favourites, especially in 2025, suggest that this preference became slightly more flexible rather than being replaced altogether.

In this kind of representation, the most informative patterns are usually in the lower-order coefficients near the bottom of the plot, because they summarize the rough shape of the spectral envelope, while the higher coefficients often capture finer detail and tend to look more stable or less visually prominent. That is also what appears here: all three songs are dominated by activity in the lower rows, but they differ in how stable, striped, or contrastive those rows are over time. Blinding Lights looks the most even and smoothed, with relatively persistent horizontal bands and fewer abrupt timbral disruptions; Dolla Dolla Bill shows stronger local fluctuation, especially in the lower coefficients, with more visible vertical striping and sectional contrast; and NUEVAYoL appears comparatively restrained for much of the track, but with a more noticeable timbral shift in the later middle section, where the lower coefficients darken and the spectral envelope seems to change more clearly. Read together, the three plots suggest not just different songs, but different ways of organizing timbre: stable and polished in Blinding Lights, more contrastive and segmented in Dolla Dolla Bill, and generally softer or more muted with a marked internal change in NUEVAYoL. MFCCs are commonly used this way because they correlate with aspects of timbre and are well suited to distinguishing sections with different instrumentation or texture.

Comparing these timbre plots with the loudness-by-year graph makes the contrast even more useful. The loudness plot showed that 2020 sits near the louder end of your yearly favourites, 2021 is somewhat quieter but still close to its year’s central range, and 2025 is the strongest low-loudness outlier, with a much quieter #1 song than the earlier yearly winners. The MFCC plots partly support that story, but not in a simple one-to-one way. NUEVAYoL’s quieter loudness is consistent with a timbral profile that looks less aggressively contrastive overall, especially compared with the brighter, denser lower-coefficient activity in Blinding Lights. At the same time, Dolla Dolla Bill shows that a track can be less loud on average yet still have a timbral pattern that is quite active and sectionally differentiated. That matters analytically because it suggests that average loudness and timbral organization are related but not identical dimensions of taste: the loudness graph points to a recent openness to quieter tracks, while the MFCC plots show that this quieter preference does not necessarily mean flatter or simpler sonic design. A careful conclusion, then, is that your listening history appears fairly stable in general intensity, but the three examples reveal meaningful variation in how sonic colour is shaped over time, from polished continuity to stronger sectional contrast to quieter, more subdued timbral space. MFCCs are useful for this comparison, but they remain feature representations rather than direct perceptual truth, so they are best read as evidence of changing spectral texture rather than as a complete description of what the songs “sound like.”

Corpus-level temporal structure

These temporal-feature plots suggest that my listening history was highly stable in metre but somewhat more flexible in song length. The time-signature chart is dominated almost entirely by 4/4 in every year, and all yearly #1 songs also fall into that metre. This means there is little evidence that my taste changed through shifts in basic pulse organization: even when other musical features varied, my favourites remained rooted in the standard quadruple metre that characterizes much popular music.

Track duration shows slightly more variation. Most yearly distributions stay centred around the familiar 3–4 minute range, which suggests another broad continuity in my listening habits, but some later years include more long-duration outliers. The yearly #1 songs usually sit near the middle of their distributions, although 2021 is relatively short, 2022 somewhat longer, and 2025 falls below the centre of a year that otherwise contains several extended tracks. Overall, these graphs suggest that my temporal preferences were more stable in metre than in length: the pulse framework stayed consistent, while the amount of time a song occupied became a little more flexible.

These novelty curves add a finer-grained temporal view to the earlier time-signature and duration plots. A novelty function highlights moments of local change, so the main thing to look for is where peaks occur, how tall they are, and whether they are evenly spread or clustered. In Dolla Dolla Bill, the first half of the excerpt is relatively quiet, but after roughly 14 seconds the curve becomes much denser and more jagged, with many high peaks. That suggests a passage with more frequent onsets or sharper local changes. Ojitos Lindos looks more even and restrained: the peaks are generally lower, more dispersed, and less dramatically clustered, which suggests a smoother local rhythmic flow. NUEVAYoL is the most contrastive of the three, because it stays fairly low for most of the excerpt and then produces a strong burst of large peaks in the final third, especially after about 23–24 seconds. Read musically, these three excerpts do not mainly differ in metre, but in how change and accent density are distributed over time. Novelty curves are useful for this because they capture local transitions and can support segmentation or tempo-related analysis even when the metre itself remains constant.

This matters for the broader temporal story because the earlier plots showed that my listening is overwhelmingly rooted in 4/4 and only moderately variable in total duration. These novelty curves show what those track-level summaries cannot: songs can share the same metre and sit in a similar overall duration range, yet still differ a great deal in sectional pacing and local rhythmic energy. In that sense, the novelty analysis refines the temporal-feature section: the stability of 4/4 suggests continuity in basic pulse organization, but the different peak patterns in these excerpts show that my favourites vary in how strongly they articulate change within that framework. Ojitos Lindos suggests a steadier and less abrupt temporal profile, while Dolla Dolla Bill and especially NUEVAYoL create stronger local contrast through clustered bursts of novelty. The safest conclusion, then, is that my taste stayed stable at the level of metre, somewhat flexible at the level of length, and more varied at the level of moment-to-moment temporal articulation and sectional contrast.

These ACT tempograms refine the earlier temporal analysis by showing how strongly different local pulse rates are supported over time. In a tempogram, brighter horizontal bands indicate a stronger locally periodic pulse at that BPM, but these bands should not be read as one exact “true tempo” only: pulse is hierarchical, so related levels such as tactus, measure, and faster subdivisions can all appear at once. Autocorrelation-based tempograms are especially useful here because they capture local periodic self-similarities in the novelty curve and often highlight slower pulse levels as well as the main beat. In Dolla Dolla Bill, the most visible bands stay relatively stable across the full track, especially around a slower pulse region near 55 BPM and its related higher levels, which suggests a fairly consistent periodic framework even when the novelty curve becomes much denser after about 14 seconds. Ojitos Lindos looks more segmented: the stronger tempo bands appear in clearer blocks, with repeated local emphasis around the same pulse region but more obvious sectional interruptions. NUEVAYoL shows the greatest late change: the tempogram becomes more active and redistributed in the later portion of the song, which matches the novelty curve’s strong burst of peaks after roughly 23–24 seconds. Read together, the tempograms suggest that these tracks do not mainly differ in basic metre, but in how steadily or flexibly they sustain local pulse energy over time.

This fits well with the earlier time-signature and duration plots. Those graphs showed that my listening history is overwhelmingly rooted in 4/4 and usually stays within a familiar 3–4 minute song-length range, so the broad temporal framework of my preferences is quite stable. The tempograms and novelty curves add the detail that those track-level summaries cannot show: songs can share the same metre and similar duration, yet still differ substantially in sectional pacing, pulse salience, and local rhythmic articulation. In that sense, Dolla Dolla Bill combines a stable pulse framework with more abrupt local change, Ojitos Lindos combines stable metre with smoother and more sectionally patterned pulse organization, and NUEVAYoL keeps the same broad temporal framework but introduces much stronger late contrast in both novelty and tempo-related energy. The strongest overall conclusion is therefore that my temporal taste was stable at the level of metre, somewhat flexible in length, and most variable in the internal organization of pulse and change over time.

Cluster distribution and interpretation

This clustered heatmap should be read in two directions at once. Each row is a song and each column is one track-level feature: acousticness, instrumentalness, tempo, duration, valence, danceability, speechiness, liveness, energy, and loudness. The colours show whether a song is above or below the overall average for each feature, with brighter cells meaning relatively higher values and darker cells meaning relatively lower values. The dendrogram on the side groups songs that have similar feature profiles, so songs placed next to each other are more alike across the full set of variables, not just in one column. The thin coloured strip next to the rows marks the year, which is useful for checking whether songs from the same year cluster together or whether songs from different years mix.

What is most noticeable is that the rows are not organized into clear year blocks. Songs from different years appear throughout the same clusters, which suggests that my listening history returns to similar feature combinations across time rather than splitting into sharply separate phases. A good example is the top part of the heatmap, where NUEVAYoL and The Lamp Is Low cluster together because they are relatively quiet, low-energy, and more instrumental/acoustic than much of the rest of the corpus. Other clusters contain songs with stronger danceability or more positive valence, showing that my taste contains several recurring feature profiles rather than one single type of song. So the important thing to notice is not just which songs are neighbours, but which columns are bright or dark within each cluster, because that reveals what kind of musical profile each group represents.

What this analysis shows

Across the full corpus, my top songs are not randomly distributed across musical feature space. Instead, they repeatedly return to a limited set of feature combinations, especially in terms of energy, danceability, tempo, and tonal organisation.

At the same time, the analyses suggest that these preferences do not remain completely fixed. Some features remain quite stable across the years, while others shift more clearly. In particular, pitch, timbre, and temporal analyses suggest gradual changes in the character of my preferred songs rather than a complete break between early and later listening habits.

Taken together, the results suggest that my music taste changed over time, but in a gradual way. The strongest pattern is not total replacement of one style by another, but a rebalancing of existing preferences: some core tendencies remain, while the relative importance of mood, texture, tonal emphasis, and rhythmic structure changes across years.

This type of analysis could be useful for anyone interested in computational musicology as a way of describing personal listening history, especially when combining corpus-level summaries with close reading of selected tracks.
