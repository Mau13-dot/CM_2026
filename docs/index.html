Project overview

This dashboard presents a computational musicological analysis of my own top songs from 2018 to 2025, using a merged corpus based on my yearly “Your Top Songs” playlists. It asks which musical features remain stable across my listening history, which change over time, and whether these changes suggest gradual shifts in taste or more substantial stylistic reorientation.

The project is guided by three research questions:

  1. What are the most common musical features across my top songs?
  2. How do these features evolve over time in my listening habits?
  3. Did my tastes change over the years, and if so, how?

The merged corpus is used for broad comparisons across years, while the yearly #1 songs serve as the main close-reading examples in the more detailed pitch, timbre, harmonic, and temporal analyses. Together, these different levels of analysis make it possible to compare overall listening patterns with close musical detail.

Main findings

Across 2018–2025, my listening history appears more stable than volatile. The strongest continuities emerge in metre, tempo, danceability, and broad feature space, while the clearest variation appears in valence, timbre, and local temporal articulation. Overall, the analyses suggest that changes in taste were mostly gradual rather than abrupt, with a few standout tracks acting as exceptions rather than evidence of a complete stylistic break.

Corpus overview

Before turning to individual feature domains, this corpus overview shows the overall balance of the dataset and highlights the broad listening patterns that already appear at the track level.

The corpus is fairly evenly distributed across the years 2018–2025, which makes year-by-year comparisons more reliable, even if some years may still differ slightly in representativeness. Loudness and timbre

This section examines two related but distinct dimensions of my listening history: overall loudness and timbral organization. At the corpus level, the loudness plot asks whether my preferred songs became louder, softer, or more variable across years. At the track level, MFCC heatmaps and timbre self-similarity matrices show how sonic colour is distributed within selected songs and how consistently that colour is repeated across sections.

Taken together, these analyses suggest that my listening history is relatively stable in overall loudness, but more varied in timbral texture and timbral form. In other words, the general intensity profile of my favourite songs remains fairly consistent across years, while the way songs shape and repeat their sonic colour differs more clearly from track to track.

Loudness in the corpus

This plot suggests that the loudness of my preferred songs was mostly stable over time, since the yearly medians remain in a fairly narrow range and the distributions overlap strongly from 2018 to 2024. That means there is little evidence of a steady shift toward either consistently louder or consistently softer listening across most of the dataset. The most noticeable differences instead appear in the spread of the yearly distributions and in the position of the #1 songs.

In most years, the favourite track sits close to the centre of that year’s loudness range, which suggests that the standout song was usually loudness-typical of my broader listening. 2025 is the clearest exception: its #1 song sits well below the yearly core, and the year also contains more very quiet outliers. This points less to a complete change in taste than to a slightly wider openness to softer tracks in recent years.

At the same time, loudness should not be equated with musical intensity more broadly. A track can have a moderate average loudness and still feel powerful because of its arrangement, density, or the way energy is distributed across sections. For that reason, this graph is most useful for showing that my listening habits stayed anchored in a fairly consistent loudness profile, while becoming somewhat more flexible at the quieter end.

Temporal features [duration]

This section examines whether my listening history changed in its temporal organization: not only in broad track-level properties such as metre and duration, but also in the finer internal pacing of songs. At the corpus level, the analysis asks whether my favourite tracks shifted toward different time signatures or song lengths across years. At the track level, novelty curves and tempograms show how strongly songs differ in local rhythmic change, sectional pacing, and pulse organization.

Taken together, these analyses suggest that my listening history was highly stable in metre, somewhat more flexible in duration, and most variable in the internal organization of pulse and change over time. In other words, the broad temporal framework of my favourites remained quite consistent, while the way songs articulated movement and contrast within that framework differed more clearly from track to track.

Corpus-level temporal structure

These temporal-feature plots suggest that my listening history was highly stable in metre but somewhat more flexible in song length. The time-signature chart is dominated almost entirely by 4/4 in every year, and all yearly #1 songs also fall into that metre. This means there is little evidence that my taste changed through shifts in basic pulse organization: even when other musical features varied, my favourites remained rooted in the standard quadruple metre that characterizes much popular music.

Track duration shows slightly more variation. Most yearly distributions stay centred around the familiar 3–4 minute range, which suggests another broad continuity in my listening habits, but some later years include more long-duration outliers. The yearly #1 songs usually sit near the middle of their distributions, although 2021 is relatively short, 2022 somewhat longer, and 2025 falls below the centre of a year that otherwise contains several extended tracks. Overall, these graphs suggest that my temporal preferences were more stable in metre than in length: the pulse framework stayed consistent, while the amount of time a song occupied became a little more flexible.

This corpus-level stability is important because it sets up the more detailed analyses on the right. If the broad metric frame remains mostly unchanged, then differences in temporal taste are more likely to appear in how songs distribute change and pulse internally than in whether they use unusual metres or radically different durations.

These ACT tempograms refine the temporal analysis further by showing how strongly different local pulse rates are supported over time. The important thing to notice is whether the brighter horizontal bands stay relatively stable or become redistributed across the track. NUEVAYoL shows the greatest late change: the tempogram becomes more active and redistributed in the later portion of the song, which matches the novelty curve’s strong burst of peaks after roughly 23–24 seconds.

Read together with the novelty curves and the corpus-level plots, the tempograms suggest that these tracks do not mainly differ in basic metre, but in how steadily or flexibly they sustain local pulse energy over time. In that sense, Dolla Dolla Bill combines a stable pulse framework with more abrupt local change, Ojitos Lindos combines stable metre with smoother and more sectionally patterned pulse organization, and NUEVAYoL keeps the same broad temporal framework but introduces much stronger late contrast in both novelty and tempo-related energy. Across the page as a whole, the strongest conclusion is that my temporal taste was stable at the level of metre, somewhat flexible in length, and most variable in the internal organization of pulse and change over time.

What this analysis shows

Taken together, the analyses suggest that my listening history is shaped more by continuity with variation than by abrupt stylistic breaks. At the broadest level, my top songs repeatedly return to a similar track-level feature space marked by relatively stable tempo, danceability, energy, and metre. The clustering analyses reinforce this pattern: songs from different years often group together rather than forming sharply separated yearly phases, which suggests that my taste remained anchored in a limited number of recurring musical profiles. In that sense, the strongest answer to the first research question is that my favourite songs are not randomly distributed across the corpus, but consistently return to a recognisable set of rhythmic, energetic, and tonal tendencies.

At the same time, the project also shows that this stability was not complete. The clearest variation appears in features related to valence, timbre, local temporal articulation, and tonal organization within songs. The close readings of the yearly #1 songs show this especially clearly. The pitch analyses suggest that some tonal centres recur across years, but also that these standout tracks differ in how tightly pitch material is concentrated, how strongly harmonic material is repeated, and how stable local key regions remain over time. The loudness and timbre analyses suggest that overall intensity stayed fairly consistent, while sonic colour and timbral repetition varied more clearly across selected yearly favourites. The temporal analyses lead to a similar conclusion: metre remained highly stable, but the #1 songs differed more in sectional pacing, novelty, and pulse organization. Together, these results suggest that changes in taste were less about replacing one musical world with another and more about rebalancing existing preferences — especially in mood, texture, tonal focus, and internal rhythmic shape.

The overall contribution of this portfolio is therefore twofold. First, it shows how computational musicology can describe a personal listening history in a way that is more precise than anecdotal self-description alone. Second, it demonstrates the value of combining corpus-level summaries with close readings of the yearly #1 songs: the larger corpus reveals broad continuity, while the detailed pitch, timbre, and temporal analyses show where meaningful differences emerge within that stable frame. This kind of approach could be useful for people interested in computational musicology, personal listening-history research, or reflective analyses of how musical taste develops over time. At the same time, the conclusions should remain cautious: all of these findings depend on the available corpus, the selected features, and the fact that track-level descriptors simplify musical experience. Even so, the main result is clear: my music taste did change across the years, but mostly through gradual shifts in emphasis within a stable preference structure, rather than through complete stylistic transformation.
